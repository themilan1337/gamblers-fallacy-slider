How much has the learner already seen? A rational account of the gambler's fallacy

A person who is not sure whether a process is independent will, on average, expect a streak to switch after a few repeats and to continue after many. How soon that shift comes depends on how many outcomes the person has already seen. This page lets you move that number.

An independent implementation of the model in Kevin Dorst, “Bayesians Commit the Gambler's Fallacy”, Cognitive Science 50(1), e70171 (2026). Built by Milan Gorislavets and Kamilya Mukh, who answer for any mistakes. Not written or endorsed by the paper's author.

The idea in four lines

  • A learner watches a process with a known 50% hit rate (a coin, a parking spot that goes to one of two cars). It does not know whether the process is Steady (independent), Switchy (a streak makes a switch more likely) or Sticky (a streak makes a continuation more likely).
  • The learner starts equally unsure between the three, then updates by Bayes' rule on every outcome it has seen. The data really do come from a fair coin.
  • Fair-coin data rule out Sticky faster than Switchy, so on average the learner leans Switchy. That lean makes it expect a switch after a short streak.
  • A long streak is itself evidence for Sticky. Past some length the average learner expects a continuation. With less experience that point comes sooner and the expectation gets stronger.

What the model does and does not claim

  • It claims (the paper's argument) that a gambler's-fallacy-like expectation is what a rational learner would have if it were unsure whether the process is independent and had seen limited data. The numbers on this page are simulation results of that learner, not measurements of people.
  • It predicts, qualitatively, that with less experience the average learner shifts sooner from expecting a switch to expecting a continuation and expects it more strongly. The paper reports the same pattern in adult studies that compare familiar processes (a coin, an urn) with unfamiliar ones (a firm's earnings, a stock), and reads “familiar” as “more data” (its Section 5.2).
  • How far the average learner dips below 50% is not steady in experience. With the default settings the dip is deepest at a moderate amount of experience (about 25 outcomes): a learner with none has no lean toward Switchy yet, and a learner with a lot has become confident of Steady. What shrinks steadily with experience is the pull toward continuations after long streaks.
  • It does not say how many outcomes any real adult has seen, and the paper does not fit its parameters to data. Which amount of experience counts as “familiar” depends on how easy the process is to learn about.
  • It does not model working memory. The experience here is the length of the sequence the learner has conditioned on, remembered perfectly. The paper's separate limited-memory version (its Section 4, “unpacking”) is not on this page.
  • The paper's other simulations (probability estimates versus binary predictions, producing random sequences, other hit rates) are not on this page. The prior and chain settings above go beyond the paper's defaults only where the paper itself varies them (its Section 6).

Choices the paper leaves open

  • How a sequence starts: the first outcome has probability 0.5 under every hypothesis, and each sequence the learner sees is treated as a fresh run of the chain.
  • How a streak is shown. The default (one miss, then s hits) reproduces the paper's Fig. 6 curves for 25- and 40-outcome learners to within about 0.01 as read by eye. The other option averages over all 8-outcome IID sequences that end in a streak of s.
  • “Uniformly unsure” over speeds 1 to 10 is read as equal weight on all 21 chains, Steady included (1/21). That reading gives 16% and 7.5% where the paper reports about 17% and 8% in its Section 4.
  • The averages are over 1000 simulated learners (the same seeded coin flips every time), so curves carry a sampling error of about 0.002 in probability.
